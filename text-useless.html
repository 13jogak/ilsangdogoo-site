Writing · On Uselessness

The useless tree guards the hill

There's a saying I keep coming back to: the ugly tree is the one that guards the hill. It goes back to Zhuangzi — the tree too crooked to be timber is the tree nobody comes for, so it stands there and keeps standing.

The spring they started building on the hill behind my studio, a lot of trees came down, and among them was a cherry tree that had stood there a long time. I brought it back with me. Of everything that came off that tree, the bark was counted the most useless of all — not timber, not even firewood.

I dried the inner bark slowly and fed it lacquer, coat after fine coat. I made it into a chaha (茶荷), a small place to set dry tea leaves down before they are brewed.

It left my hands years ago, and then one day I met it again in a photograph. It had let go of even the smell of the lacquer and settled into everyday use, there for the tea and nothing else. I was glad. The ugly tree that guarded the hill for so long had found a good seat.

The years a tree has lived stay in its bark exactly as they were. All the time the bark was of no use to anyone, it was standing between the tree and the weather, keeping those years. The lacquer holds them there a while longer.

Useful and useless. The harmful and the harmless. A stumbling stone and a stepping stone. Indifference and love. The everyday and the timeless. In the short gap between summer and winter, I try to build a bridge.

So when a piece of bark has been sitting in my studio for years, the question I ask isn't what it is good for. It's what comes after usefulness — 쓸모 그 다음. I don't always have an answer. I keep the wood anyway.
